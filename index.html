New Introduction
New

Vibe coding governance is the set of policies and platform controls that let employees build apps with AI while IT keeps visibility and control.

Most builders sit outside engineering, in operations, finance, HR and product management. They describe what they want in natural language, and code review never sees their work.

Banning vibe coding doesn't stop it. It pushes it onto personal accounts and unapproved tools, which is shadow IT with an AI engine attached. IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 found that 20% of organizations studied had a breach involving shadow AI, and high levels of shadow AI added an average of $670,000 per breach.

The workable answer for enterprise vibe coding is a sanctioned platform with the guardrails built in. This guide covers what to govern, how to tier risk, which controls to enforce, who owns what and how to roll it out in 90 days.

New What vibe coding governance actually means
New

What vibe coding governance actually means

Governing vibe coding means setting rules for apps that people build by describing them to an AI, where the person who builds the app may never read the code behind it. The rules have to work without that person's review, so they belong in the platform the apps are built and published on.

How it differs from governing AI coding assistants

Engineers who use AI coding assistants still read diffs, run tests and send changes through code review. That is AI-assisted development, and your existing software development lifecycle mostly covers it. The engineer stays accountable for every line.

Vibe coding removes that reviewer. An operations analyst who builds a vendor-tracking app judges it by whether it works. How it's built is invisible to them. The AI-generated code can be correct and still ship with a hardcoded key or an open endpoint. Policies that assume someone will read the code don't apply, so citizen developer governance for vibe coding has to live in the platform.

Sanctioned platform, unsanctioned app

Approving a platform doesn't approve every app built on it. The Cloud Security Alliance's research note The Vibe Coding Governance Gap puts it plainly: "The platform is sanctioned; the application is not." The risk now lives inside approved tools, in apps that nobody in IT has seen.

Put it into practice: write one sentence that defines a governed app in your organization, such as "any app built with AI that runs on company data or is shared with more than one person." Every policy in the rest of this guide hangs off that definition.

New Where vibe coding breaks down at scale
New

Where vibe coding breaks down at scale

Vibe coding breaks down at scale when apps multiply faster than anyone can track them, go live without review and outlive the people who built them. These six governance risks show up in almost every enterprise vibe coding program.

Shadow apps and no inventory

You can't secure an app you don't know exists. Without a registry, a security team can't answer basic questions: how many apps are live, who built them and what data they read. Incident response starts with a hunt for the app.

Duplicate apps and sprawl

When building takes an afternoon, teams rebuild before they look for what exists. Two product management teams build two feature-request trackers, three regions end up with three expense trackers, each with its own logic and its own copy of the data. Every one-off adds snowflake complexity and future technical debt management work.

Unreviewed publishing

A vibe-coded app can go from prompt to public URL in minutes, and its security vulnerabilities go live with it. The common gaps are predictable: API keys in client code, missing sign-in, database tables without row-level security, and open preview links. GitGuardian's State of Secrets Sprawl 2026 counted 28.65 million new hardcoded secrets in public GitHub commits in 2025, a 34% increase over the year before. The CSA note also reports that Georgia Tech's Vibe Security Radar tracked 6 CVEs attributed to AI-generated code in January 2026, 15 in February and 35 in March.

Sensitive data in prompts and connectors

Builders paste customer records into prompts and connect apps to CRMs, warehouses and inboxes. Each connection is a new data flow that your data protection team never mapped. Generative AI also makes it easy to send that data to an outside model without anyone noticing.

Orphaned apps with no owner

The builder changes roles or leaves. The app keeps running, keeps its access and keeps processing data. Nobody patches its dependencies, and nobody knows whether anyone still uses it.

Compliance blind spots

GDPR and HIPAA obligations follow the data an app handles. How the app was built doesn't change them. A vibe-coded app that touches health records carries the same HIPAA exposure as one built by engineers. The EU AI Act adds rules for high-risk AI systems. Under the current EU timeline, the Annex III high-risk rules apply from December 2, 2027, after the Digital Omnibus on AI moved the original August 2, 2026 date. An app that screens job candidates or scores credit could fall into that category.

Put it into practice: score your organization against these six points today. Each one you can't answer becomes a line item in the rollout plan below.

New A tiered governance model
New

A tiered governance model

A tiered model matches the depth of review to the risk of the app, so a personal prototype moves fast while an app that touches regulated data gets a full security review. Three tiers cover most cases.

TierExamplesRequired controlsWho reviewsPublish rule
Tier 1: Personal or prototypeA proof of concept (PoC), a personal calculator, a demo for one meetingCompany sign-in, private by default, automated scan, no production dataNo one. The builder self-certifiesPrivate only. Register or delete within 30 days
Tier 2: Internal team tool, non-sensitive dataInternal tools like a team dashboard, a request intake form or a product management trackerTier 1 controls plus registry entry, named owner, role-based access, passing scan, Git-tracked changesPlatform admin checks the registry entry and scan resultPrivate deployment inside the company. Publishing blocked on failed scan
Tier 3: Regulated data, customer-facing or agenticApps that touch PII, health or payment data, apps for customers, apps that call AI APIs or act on external systemsTier 2 controls plus security review, data protection review, input validation and output checks, logged connectors, approval workflowsIT/security and the data ownerPublic only after sign-off. Re-review on major changes

Tiers keep enterprise vibe coding fast where risk is low. They should also be easy to assign. Ask three questions at intake: whose data does it touch, who will use it and can it take actions outside the app? The highest answer sets the tier.

Put it into practice: publish the tier table internally, add the three intake questions to your registry form and set Tier 1 as the default for every new app.

New The control layers
New

The control layers

Six control layers cover most vibe coding security risk: identity and access, environment isolation, pre-publish gates, data and secrets, visibility and audit, and lifecycle and ownership. Each layer should be enforced by the platform, so the builder doesn't have to remember it. Together they form the security controls an auditor will ask about.

Identity and access

Tie every builder and every app user to your identity provider. Require single sign-on, provision and remove users automatically, and map identity provider groups to roles. Use role-based access so a viewer can open an app without being able to edit or publish it. These access controls are the base that every other security control depends on.

Environment isolation

Keep development and production apart. Preview links should require company sign-in, production data shouldn't be reachable from a prototype, and each app should only see its own data. Isolation limits the blast radius when one app has a flaw. Replit, for example, runs each app in its own cloud sandbox with a separate frontend and backend.

Pre-publish gates

Publishing is the one moment every app passes through, so it's the right place for checks. Require an automated scan before any app goes live, block publishing when findings cross a severity threshold, and require changes to be pushed to a Git remote first. The gate runs the same way for every builder.

Data and secrets

Keep credentials out of code and out of prompts. Store keys in a secrets manager that injects them at runtime, scope each connector to the apps that need it and limit which AI models and providers teams can use. Treat prompt content as data that may hold personal information. On Replit, connectors such as the Databricks integration let apps query governed tables in place, without copying sensitive data into the app.

Visibility and audit

Log who built, changed, published and accessed each app, and stream those events to your SIEM. Audit trails turn an incident from guesswork into a timeline. Pair them with a usage view that shows every published app, its creator and whether it's public or private.

Lifecycle and ownership

Every app needs a named owner, a review date and a way to retire it. When an owner leaves, ownership moves to their team. Unused apps get unpublished on a schedule. After launch, rescan published apps when a new vulnerability affects their dependencies, and keep a fast way to unpublish an app and revoke its connectors during an incident. Move a team tool to engineering ownership once the business depends on it, for example when it handles customer or regulated data or a team can't work without it. Replit backs up every filesystem at least daily, which gives owners a recovery point if a change goes wrong. This layer is what keeps maintainability from falling apart over time.

Put it into practice: list the six layers in a table and mark each one as enforced by the platform, enforced by process or not covered. Close the "not covered" rows first.

Why: Six vendor-neutral layers, each in its own H3, give IT leaders a checklist they can map to any platform. Three short Replit examples (sandboxes, the Databricks connector, daily backups) show a control in practice without repeating the Replit table, and the lifecycle layer now covers rescans, incident unpublishing and handoff to engineering. New How to prevent duplicate apps
New

How to prevent duplicate apps

Prevent duplicate apps by making existing apps easy to find before anyone builds a new one. Keep a central registry with an owner, purpose and data tier for every app, add a reuse check to intake and give teams shared workspaces and templates, so the second team extends the first team's app instead of starting over.

  • Discovery before build. Make search the first step of the intake form. A builder describes the problem, sees similar apps and can ask the owner for access before starting.
  • A central registry. Track every Tier 2 and Tier 3 app in one place: name, owner, team, purpose, data sources, tier and review date. A shared sheet works to start. Many teams later build the registry as an internal app of its own.
  • Shared workspaces and templates. Put teams in shared workspaces where they can see each other's projects. Publish approved starting points, like a standard dashboard layout or an intake form pattern, so new apps start from vetted parts.
  • Naming and ownership metadata. Use a naming pattern such as team, function and app name, for example "finance-ap-invoice-tracker." Require an owner and a backup owner before an app can move past Tier 1.
  • Reuse-first intake. For Tier 2 and Tier 3 requests, ask one question before approval: does something similar already exist? If it does, the default answer is to extend it.

Put it into practice: run a one-time sweep of published apps, group them by purpose and merge or retire the obvious duplicates. Then turn on the reuse check so the sweep doesn't need repeating.

New Security checks to run before publishing
New

Security checks to run before publishing

Before a vibe-coded app is published, run automated checks for exposed secrets, missing sign-in, weak access policies, unvalidated input, vulnerable dependencies and public visibility settings, and require every change to be tracked in Git. Enforce these checks in the platform, so publishing stops when a check fails.

  • Secrets and API keys. Scan the code and client bundle for hardcoded keys, tokens and passwords. Move every credential to a secrets manager.
  • Authentication and authorization. Confirm that every page and API route requires sign-in where it should, and that users can only reach their own records.
  • Access policies and row-level security. Check database permissions. Tables that hold user data need policies that limit each user to their own rows.
  • Input validation. Validate and sanitize form fields, query parameters and file uploads to block SQL injection and cross-site scripting.
  • Dependency vulnerabilities. Check every package against known CVEs and block publishing above your severity threshold.
  • Security headers and CSRF. Set standard headers like a content security policy, and protect state-changing requests against cross-site request forgery.
  • Privacy and visibility settings. Confirm the app publishes as private unless it has an approved exception, and that preview URLs require sign-in.
  • Agentic and AI risk. For apps that call AI models or act on other systems, test for prompt injection, give the app least-privilege permissions on each system it touches and require human approval for irreversible actions, like deleting records or sending payments.
  • Git-tracked changes and review. Push changes to a Git remote before publishing, so every version has a record and Tier 3 apps can get a human review.

A clean scan is a floor. Tier 3 apps still need a human security review that looks at how the app's parts connect, because scanners catch code-level flaws more reliably than design flaws. For builder-facing advice, see 16 ways to vibe code securely.

Put it into practice: turn this list into your publish gate. Automate what scanners catch reliably, like secrets, dependencies and visibility settings, and review the rest during tier approval.

Roles and ownership

Clear ownership keeps vibe-coded apps from becoming orphans: IT and security set policy, platform admins enforce it, builders follow it and a named app owner stays accountable after launch. The RACI below assigns each activity.

ActivityIT/securityPlatform adminsBuildersApp owners
Set tier policy and publish rulesA/RCII
Configure platform controlsARII
Register a new app and assign its tierICRA
Run pre-publish checksIARC
Review Tier 3 appsA/RCCR
Respond to vulnerabilitiesARCR
Review access and usage each quarterARIR
Retire or transfer an appIRCA

R = responsible, A = accountable, C = consulted, I = informed.

The app owner is often the builder, but not always. For a team tool, the owner should be a manager who will still be on that team in a year.

Put it into practice: add an owner field to your registry and make it required. An app without an owner can't move past Tier 1.

New A 30/60/90-day rollout
New

A 30/60/90-day rollout

You can stand up vibe coding governance in 90 days by starting with visibility, then adding a tiered policy, then moving enforcement into the platform. Each phase builds on the one before it.

Days 1 to 30: visibility and lightweight registration

  • Pick one sanctioned platform and connect it to your identity provider.
  • Inventory what's already live: published apps, their creators and their visibility.
  • Launch a short registration form with owner, purpose, data sources and tier.
  • Announce the program as a way to get support for builders, so people have a reason to register their apps.

Days 31 to 60: tiered policy

  • Publish the three tiers and the intake questions.
  • Name app owners for every registered Tier 2 and Tier 3 app.
  • Set up review paths: platform admins for Tier 2, IT/security and data owners for Tier 3.
  • Run the duplicate sweep and retire or merge what you find.

Days 61 to 90: enforcement at the platform layer

  • Require private deployments by default, with documented exceptions.
  • Require a scan before publishing and block on high-severity findings.
  • Require a Git remote before publishing.
  • Stream audit events to your SIEM and schedule quarterly access reviews.

Put it into practice: assign one owner for each phase and put the day-90 settings on the calendar now. Enforcement that isn't scheduled tends to slip.

Why: A phased plan turns the model into dated work. The order (visibility, then policy, then enforcement) follows the CSA note's own recommendations. Sources: n/a

A policy applies across workspaces, and builders follow it by default. That matches the CSA note's own conclusion: platform vendors should provide baseline controls, such as mandatory security policy templates, automatic secrets scanning at deploy time and required authentication configuration, that work "without requiring citizen developers to possess security knowledge they do not have."

New FAQ
New

Frequently asked questions

How do you find vibe-coded apps that are already running?

Start with what your platforms already record: published apps, their creators and whether each one is public. Audit logs and expense reports for AI tool subscriptions show where building happens outside approved tools. Then open a short registration form and tell builders that registering gets them support.

Do builders need security training to vibe code safely?

A short orientation on which data they can use and how the tiers work covers most builders. The heavy lifting belongs in the platform: required sign-in, private publishing by default and scans that block risky apps. Save deeper training for owners of Tier 3 apps.

How long does a vibe coding governance rollout take?

The plan in this guide takes 90 days. Visibility and registration come first, a tiered policy follows in the second month, and platform enforcement starts in the third. If you already have one sanctioned platform tied to your identity provider, you can start the third phase sooner.

Should you ban vibe coding?

Bans tend to push building onto personal accounts and unapproved tools, where IT has no visibility at all. A sanctioned platform with clear tiers gives builders an approved path. Restrict specific risks instead, like public publishing or connections to regulated data.

Who owns a vibe-coded app?

A named app owner is accountable for each app, even when someone else built it. For team tools, the owner should be a manager who will stay with the team. When an owner leaves, ownership moves to their team, and the app is reviewed or retired.

Is vibe coding compatible with SOC 2 and GDPR?

Yes, if vibe-coded apps fall under the same controls as your other systems: access control, audit trails, change tracking and data mapping. The obligations follow the data, so an app that handles personal data needs the same records as any other. Replit has achieved a SOC 2 Type 2 attestation for its own platform. Each app built on it still needs its own controls.

Governing vibe coding at scale: an enterprise guide

Published: Nov 2, 2026Updated: Nov 2, 2026The Replit Team
[Cover image: Replit admin settings with security, privacy and source control policies]

Vibe coding governance is the set of policies and platform controls that let employees build apps with AI while IT keeps visibility and control.

Most builders sit outside engineering, in operations, finance, HR and product management. They describe what they want in natural language, and code review never sees their work.

Banning vibe coding doesn't stop it. It pushes it onto personal accounts and unapproved tools, which is shadow IT with an AI engine attached. IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 found that 20% of organizations studied had a breach involving shadow AI, and high levels of shadow AI added an average of $670,000 per breach.

The workable answer for enterprise vibe coding is a sanctioned platform with the guardrails built in. This guide covers what to govern, how to tier risk, which controls to enforce, who owns what and how to roll it out in 90 days.

What vibe coding governance actually means

Governing vibe coding means setting rules for apps that people build by describing them to an AI, where the person who builds the app may never read the code behind it. The rules have to work without that person's review, so they belong in the platform the apps are built and published on.

How it differs from governing AI coding assistants

Engineers who use AI coding assistants still read diffs, run tests and send changes through code review. That is AI-assisted development, and your existing software development lifecycle mostly covers it. The engineer stays accountable for every line.

Vibe coding removes that reviewer. An operations analyst who builds a vendor-tracking app judges it by whether it works. How it's built is invisible to them. The AI-generated code can be correct and still ship with a hardcoded key or an open endpoint. Policies that assume someone will read the code don't apply, so citizen developer governance for vibe coding has to live in the platform.

Sanctioned platform, unsanctioned app

Approving a platform doesn't approve every app built on it. The Cloud Security Alliance's research note The Vibe Coding Governance Gap puts it plainly: "The platform is sanctioned; the application is not." The risk now lives inside approved tools, in apps that nobody in IT has seen.

Put it into practice: write one sentence that defines a governed app in your organization, such as "any app built with AI that runs on company data or is shared with more than one person." Every policy in the rest of this guide hangs off that definition.

Where vibe coding breaks down at scale

Vibe coding breaks down at scale when apps multiply faster than anyone can track them, go live without review and outlive the people who built them. These six governance risks show up in almost every enterprise vibe coding program.

Shadow apps and no inventory

You can't secure an app you don't know exists. Without a registry, a security team can't answer basic questions: how many apps are live, who built them and what data they read. Incident response starts with a hunt for the app.

Duplicate apps and sprawl

When building takes an afternoon, teams rebuild before they look for what exists. Two product management teams build two feature-request trackers, three regions end up with three expense trackers, each with its own logic and its own copy of the data. Every one-off adds snowflake complexity and future technical debt management work.

Unreviewed publishing

A vibe-coded app can go from prompt to public URL in minutes, and its security vulnerabilities go live with it. The common gaps are predictable: API keys in client code, missing sign-in, database tables without row-level security, and open preview links. GitGuardian's State of Secrets Sprawl 2026 counted 28.65 million new hardcoded secrets in public GitHub commits in 2025, a 34% increase over the year before. The CSA note also reports that Georgia Tech's Vibe Security Radar tracked 6 CVEs attributed to AI-generated code in January 2026, 15 in February and 35 in March.

Sensitive data in prompts and connectors

Builders paste customer records into prompts and connect apps to CRMs, warehouses and inboxes. Each connection is a new data flow that your data protection team never mapped. Generative AI also makes it easy to send that data to an outside model without anyone noticing.

Orphaned apps with no owner

The builder changes roles or leaves. The app keeps running, keeps its access and keeps processing data. Nobody patches its dependencies, and nobody knows whether anyone still uses it.

Compliance blind spots

GDPR and HIPAA obligations follow the data an app handles. How the app was built doesn't change them. A vibe-coded app that touches health records carries the same HIPAA exposure as one built by engineers. The EU AI Act adds rules for high-risk AI systems. Under the current EU timeline, the Annex III high-risk rules apply from December 2, 2027, after the Digital Omnibus on AI moved the original August 2, 2026 date. An app that screens job candidates or scores credit could fall into that category.

Put it into practice: score your organization against these six points today. Each one you can't answer becomes a line item in the rollout plan below.

A tiered governance model

A tiered model matches the depth of review to the risk of the app, so a personal prototype moves fast while an app that touches regulated data gets a full security review. Three tiers cover most cases.

TierExamplesRequired controlsWho reviewsPublish rule
Tier 1: Personal or prototypeA proof of concept (PoC), a personal calculator, a demo for one meetingCompany sign-in, private by default, automated scan, no production dataNo one. The builder self-certifiesPrivate only. Register or delete within 30 days
Tier 2: Internal team tool, non-sensitive dataInternal tools like a team dashboard, a request intake form or a product management trackerTier 1 controls plus registry entry, named owner, role-based access, passing scan, Git-tracked changesPlatform admin checks the registry entry and scan resultPrivate deployment inside the company. Publishing blocked on failed scan
Tier 3: Regulated data, customer-facing or agenticApps that touch PII, health or payment data, apps for customers, apps that call AI APIs or act on external systemsTier 2 controls plus security review, data protection review, input validation and output checks, logged connectors, approval workflowsIT/security and the data ownerPublic only after sign-off. Re-review on major changes

Tiers keep enterprise vibe coding fast where risk is low. They should also be easy to assign. Ask three questions at intake: whose data does it touch, who will use it and can it take actions outside the app? The highest answer sets the tier.

Put it into practice: publish the tier table internally, add the three intake questions to your registry form and set Tier 1 as the default for every new app.

The control layers

Six control layers cover most vibe coding security risk: identity and access, environment isolation, pre-publish gates, data and secrets, visibility and audit, and lifecycle and ownership. Each layer should be enforced by the platform, so the builder doesn't have to remember it. Together they form the security controls an auditor will ask about.

Identity and access

Tie every builder and every app user to your identity provider. Require single sign-on, provision and remove users automatically, and map identity provider groups to roles. Use role-based access so a viewer can open an app without being able to edit or publish it. These access controls are the base that every other security control depends on.

Environment isolation

Keep development and production apart. Preview links should require company sign-in, production data shouldn't be reachable from a prototype, and each app should only see its own data. Isolation limits the blast radius when one app has a flaw. Replit, for example, runs each app in its own cloud sandbox with a separate frontend and backend.

Pre-publish gates

Publishing is the one moment every app passes through, so it's the right place for checks. Require an automated scan before any app goes live, block publishing when findings cross a severity threshold, and require changes to be pushed to a Git remote first. The gate runs the same way for every builder.

Data and secrets

Keep credentials out of code and out of prompts. Store keys in a secrets manager that injects them at runtime, scope each connector to the apps that need it and limit which AI models and providers teams can use. Treat prompt content as data that may hold personal information. On Replit, connectors such as the Databricks integration let apps query governed tables in place, without copying sensitive data into the app.

Visibility and audit

Log who built, changed, published and accessed each app, and stream those events to your SIEM. Audit trails turn an incident from guesswork into a timeline. Pair them with a usage view that shows every published app, its creator and whether it's public or private.

Lifecycle and ownership

Every app needs a named owner, a review date and a way to retire it. When an owner leaves, ownership moves to their team. Unused apps get unpublished on a schedule. After launch, rescan published apps when a new vulnerability affects their dependencies, and keep a fast way to unpublish an app and revoke its connectors during an incident. Move a team tool to engineering ownership once the business depends on it, for example when it handles customer or regulated data or a team can't work without it. Replit backs up every filesystem at least daily, which gives owners a recovery point if a change goes wrong. This layer is what keeps maintainability from falling apart over time.

Put it into practice: list the six layers in a table and mark each one as enforced by the platform, enforced by process or not covered. Close the "not covered" rows first.

How to prevent duplicate apps

Prevent duplicate apps by making existing apps easy to find before anyone builds a new one. Keep a central registry with an owner, purpose and data tier for every app, add a reuse check to intake and give teams shared workspaces and templates, so the second team extends the first team's app instead of starting over.

  • Discovery before build. Make search the first step of the intake form. A builder describes the problem, sees similar apps and can ask the owner for access before starting.
  • A central registry. Track every Tier 2 and Tier 3 app in one place: name, owner, team, purpose, data sources, tier and review date. A shared sheet works to start. Many teams later build the registry as an internal app of its own.
  • Shared workspaces and templates. Put teams in shared workspaces where they can see each other's projects. Publish approved starting points, like a standard dashboard layout or an intake form pattern, so new apps start from vetted parts.
  • Naming and ownership metadata. Use a naming pattern such as team, function and app name, for example "finance-ap-invoice-tracker." Require an owner and a backup owner before an app can move past Tier 1.
  • Reuse-first intake. For Tier 2 and Tier 3 requests, ask one question before approval: does something similar already exist? If it does, the default answer is to extend it.

Put it into practice: run a one-time sweep of published apps, group them by purpose and merge or retire the obvious duplicates. Then turn on the reuse check so the sweep doesn't need repeating.

Security checks to run before publishing

Before a vibe-coded app is published, run automated checks for exposed secrets, missing sign-in, weak access policies, unvalidated input, vulnerable dependencies and public visibility settings, and require every change to be tracked in Git. Enforce these checks in the platform, so publishing stops when a check fails.

  • Secrets and API keys. Scan the code and client bundle for hardcoded keys, tokens and passwords. Move every credential to a secrets manager.
  • Authentication and authorization. Confirm that every page and API route requires sign-in where it should, and that users can only reach their own records.
  • Access policies and row-level security. Check database permissions. Tables that hold user data need policies that limit each user to their own rows.
  • Input validation. Validate and sanitize form fields, query parameters and file uploads to block SQL injection and cross-site scripting.
  • Dependency vulnerabilities. Check every package against known CVEs and block publishing above your severity threshold.
  • Security headers and CSRF. Set standard headers like a content security policy, and protect state-changing requests against cross-site request forgery.
  • Privacy and visibility settings. Confirm the app publishes as private unless it has an approved exception, and that preview URLs require sign-in.
  • Agentic and AI risk. For apps that call AI models or act on other systems, test for prompt injection, give the app least-privilege permissions on each system it touches and require human approval for irreversible actions, like deleting records or sending payments.
  • Git-tracked changes and review. Push changes to a Git remote before publishing, so every version has a record and Tier 3 apps can get a human review.

A clean scan is a floor. Tier 3 apps still need a human security review that looks at how the app's parts connect, because scanners catch code-level flaws more reliably than design flaws. For builder-facing advice, see 16 ways to vibe code securely.

Put it into practice: turn this list into your publish gate. Automate what scanners catch reliably, like secrets, dependencies and visibility settings, and review the rest during tier approval.

Roles and ownership

Clear ownership keeps vibe-coded apps from becoming orphans: IT and security set policy, platform admins enforce it, builders follow it and a named app owner stays accountable after launch. The RACI below assigns each activity.

The app owner is often the builder, but not always. For a team tool, the owner should be a manager who will still be on that team in a year.

Put it into practice: add an owner field to your registry and make it required. An app without an owner can't move past Tier 1.

A 30/60/90-day rollout

You can stand up vibe coding governance in 90 days by starting with visibility, then adding a tiered policy, then moving enforcement into the platform. Each phase builds on the one before it.

Days 1 to 30: visibility and lightweight registration

  • Pick one sanctioned platform and connect it to your identity provider.
  • Inventory what's already live: published apps, their creators and their visibility.
  • Launch a short registration form with owner, purpose, data sources and tier.
  • Announce the program as a way to get support for builders, so people have a reason to register their apps.

Days 31 to 60: tiered policy

  • Publish the three tiers and the intake questions.
  • Name app owners for every registered Tier 2 and Tier 3 app.
  • Set up review paths: platform admins for Tier 2, IT/security and data owners for Tier 3.
  • Run the duplicate sweep and retire or merge what you find.

Days 61 to 90: enforcement at the platform layer

  • Require private deployments by default, with documented exceptions.
  • Require a scan before publishing and block on high-severity findings.
  • Require a Git remote before publishing.
  • Stream audit events to your SIEM and schedule quarterly access reviews.

Put it into practice: assign one owner for each phase and put the day-90 settings on the calendar now. Enforcement that isn't scheduled tends to slip.

Frequently asked questions

How do you find vibe-coded apps that are already running?

Start with what your platforms already record: published apps, their creators and whether each one is public. Audit logs and expense reports for AI tool subscriptions show where building happens outside approved tools. Then open a short registration form and tell builders that registering gets them support.

Do builders need security training to vibe code safely?

A short orientation on which data they can use and how the tiers work covers most builders. The heavy lifting belongs in the platform: required sign-in, private publishing by default and scans that block risky apps. Save deeper training for owners of Tier 3 apps.

How long does a vibe coding governance rollout take?

The plan in this guide takes 90 days. Visibility and registration come first, a tiered policy follows in the second month, and platform enforcement starts in the third. If you already have one sanctioned platform tied to your identity provider, you can start the third phase sooner.

Should you ban vibe coding?

Bans tend to push building onto personal accounts and unapproved tools, where IT has no visibility at all. A sanctioned platform with clear tiers gives builders an approved path. Restrict specific risks instead, like public publishing or connections to regulated data.

Who owns a vibe-coded app?

A named app owner is accountable for each app, even when someone else built it. For team tools, the owner should be a manager who will stay with the team. When an owner leaves, ownership moves to their team, and the app is reviewed or retired.

Is vibe coding compatible with SOC 2 and GDPR?

Yes, if vibe-coded apps fall under the same controls as your other systems: access control, audit trails, change tracking and data mapping. The obligations follow the data, so an app that handles personal data needs the same records as any other. Replit has achieved a SOC 2 Type 2 attestation for its own platform. Each app built on it still needs its own controls.
